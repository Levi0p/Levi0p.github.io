Markov Decision Processes

Everything in rl sits on top of a markov decision process M = {S, A, T, r} where S is the state space, A is the action space, T is the transition operator and r is the reward function, states and actions can be discrete or continuous

The reason T is called an operator and not just a probability is that if you write μt,j = p(st = j) and ξt,k = p(at = k) and Ti,j,k = p(st+1 = i | st = j, at = k) then the next state distribution is literally just T applied to the current one

μt+1,i = Σj,k Ti,j,k μt,j ξt,k

so T is really a tensor that pushes the whole distribution forward one step

The Goal of RL

The policy πθ(a | s) picks an action, the world gives back a new state using p(s′ | s, a) and this loop runs for T steps, so the probability of a whole trajectory τ = (s1, a1, …, sT, aT) is just everything multiplied together

pθ(τ) = p(s1) Πt=1T πθ(at | st) p(st+1 | st, at)

and the goal is to find the params that make high reward trajectories most likely

θ* = argmaxθ Eτ ~ pθ(τ) [ Σt r(st, at) ]

One way to look at this is that the pair (s, a) itself forms a markov chain coz p((st+1, at+1) | (st, at)) = p(st+1 | st, at) πθ(at+1 | st+1) which lets us rewrite the objective as a sum of expectations over the state action marginal at each step

finite horizon   θ* = argmaxθ Σt=1T E(st, at) ~ pθ(st, at) [ r(st, at) ]

Infinite Horizon and the Stationary Distribution

What if T is infinite, then the sum blows up so we average it instead and the question becomes does p(st, at) settle down to some fixed distribution as t goes on, and under some regularity conditions it does and it is called the stationary distribution μ = pθ(s, a)

Stationary just means it is the same before and after a transition so μ = Tμ which means (T − I)μ = 0 ie μ is an eigenvector of T with eigenvalue 1, and once you are there the early steps barely matter and the objective just becomes the expected reward under that distribution

θ* = argmaxθ (1/T) Σt E[r(st, at)] → E(s, a) ~ pθ(s, a) [ r(s, a) ]

Why We Always Care About Expectations

In rl we almost always care about expectations and there is a really nice reason for it, think of a car on a cliff road where staying on the road is +1 and falling off is −1, the reward itself is not smooth at all coz you are either on the road or not, but if the policy falls with probability θ then the expected reward is smooth in θ

So even when the reward is some ugly step function the expected reward is a smooth function of the params and that is exactly what lets us use gradient based methods on stuff that looks totally non differentiable

The Anatomy of an RL Algorithm

Pretty much every rl algorithm is the same loop of three boxes and the differences between algorithms are just what goes in each box

The orange box is where we actually run the policy in the world and collect trajectories, the green box is where we figure out how good things are and the blue box is where we change the policy

A simple example is when the green box just estimates the return by averaging the rewards of the samples J(θ) ≈ (1/N) Σi Σt rti and the blue box does a gradient step θ ← θ + α∇θJ(θ) to make the better trajectories more likely, and a fancier one is rl by backprop where the green box learns a model fφ such that st+1 ≈ fφ(st, at) and then the blue box backprops through fφ and r to train πθ(st) = at

How expensive each box is depends a lot on the setting, generating samples on a real robot is slow and painful while in a simulator it is basically free, and the green box can be anything from summing some numbers to training a whole neural net

Q Functions and Value Functions

These two show up in every part after this so worth getting them clear

The Q function is the expected total reward from taking a specific action in a state and then following the policy after that

Qπ(st, at) = Σt′=tT Eπθ [ r(st′, at′) | st, at ]

The value function is the expected total reward from a state averaging over whatever actions the policy would choose

Vπ(st) = Eat ~ π(at | st) [ Qπ(st, at) ]

And the rl objective is just the value averaged over the possible starting states Es1 ~ p(s1)[Vπ(s1)] and rl is trying to make this number as big as possible

The two big ideas for using them are, first if we know Qπ(s, a) we can make a new policy that just picks argmaxa Qπ(s, a) and it is guaranteed to be at least as good as π and probably better no matter what π was, and second if Qπ(s, a) > Vπ(s) then a is better than average so we can push the policy to make a more likely, these two ideas basically are value based methods and policy gradients and they come back again and again

Types of Algorithms and Sample Efficiency

Value based algorithms fit V(s) or Q(s, a) in the green box and then set π(s) = argmaxa Q(s, a) in the blue box, direct policy gradients just evaluate the returns Rτ = Σt r(st, at) and take a gradient step, actor critic is a mix of both and model based rl learns how the world works and plans or trains through it

Sample efficiency is how many samples we need to get a good policy and the most important question for it is whether the algorithm is off policy, off policy means you can improve the policy without generating new samples from that policy and on policy means every time the policy changes even a little bit you have to go collect new samples

Roughly from most efficient to least it goes model based shallow rl, model based deep rl, off policy Q learning, actor critic, on policy policy gradients and then evolutionary or gradient free methods, so why would anyone use a less efficient one, coz wall clock time is not the same as efficiency and if your simulator is cheap the less efficient algo can still finish first

The other thing is that for supervised learning we usually dont have to worry if it converges coz it is almost always gradient descent but the same cant be said for rl where a lot of algorithms are not gradient descent at all, Q learning is a fixed point iteration and model based rl is optimizing a model that isnt the same as the reward, policy gradient actually is gradient descent on the real objective but it is often the least efficient one

Evaluating the Objective

Now into policy gradients which is the most direct approach, call the objective J(θ) = Eτ ~ pθ(τ)[Σt r(st, at)]

If you want to know what J(θ) is and you dont know p(s1) or p(st+1 | st, at) you just run the policy for N samples of T timesteps and average the total reward and that gets quite close to the real number

J(θ) ≈ (1/N) Σi Σt r(si,t, ai,t)

but we dont just want the value we want its gradient so we can improve it

Deriving the Policy Gradient

Write J(θ) = ∫ pθ(τ) r(τ) dτ where r(τ) is the sum of rewards on the trajectory, the gradient of that has ∇θpθ(τ) inside which we cant sample from, so we use a convenient identity

pθ(τ) ∇θ log pθ(τ) = pθ(τ) ∇θpθ(τ) / pθ(τ) = ∇θpθ(τ)

which turns the gradient back into an expectation that we can estimate with samples

∇θJ(θ) = ∫ ∇θpθ(τ) r(τ) dτ = Eτ ~ pθ(τ) [ ∇θ log pθ(τ) r(τ) ]

Now take the log of the trajectory probability and the product becomes a sum log pθ(τ) = log p(s1) + Σt [ log πθ(at | st) + log p(st+1 | st, at) ] and the best part is that p(s1) and the transitions dont depend on θ so their gradient is zero and they just disappear

∇θJ(θ) = Eτ ~ pθ(τ) [ ( Σt ∇θ log πθ(at | st) ) ( Σt r(st, at) ) ]

So we dont need to know the dynamics at all which is huge, and replacing the expectation with samples gives the REINFORCE algorithm

1. sample {τi} from πθ(at | st) ie run the policy
2. ∇θJ(θ) ≈ Σi ( Σt ∇θ log πθ(ati | sti) ) ( Σt r(sti, ati) )
3. θ ← θ + α∇θJ(θ)

Policy Gradient vs Maximum Likelihood

Maximum likelihood means adjusting the network's params to maximize the probability it assigns to the actions seen in the training data, which is exactly behavioural cloning from part 1

policy gradient   (1/N) Σi ( Σt ∇θ log πθ(ai,t | si,t) ) ( Σt r(si,t, ai,t) )
maximum likelihood   (1/N) Σi ( Σt ∇θ log πθ(ai,t | si,t) )

They use the exact same log probability gradient and the only difference is that REINFORCE weights it by the reward the trajectory got, so behaviours that led to high reward get pushed up and ones with negative reward get pushed down, you sample with the current policy, compute the gradient weighted by reward and update to maximize expected reward, so good stuff is made more likely and bad stuff less likely and it is basically just a mathematical way of writing trial and error

This also means in code you can implement it as a weighted maximum likelihood loss which is what everyone actually does

Partial Observability

If you look back at the derivation we never used the markov property anywhere, so you can just swap st with ot and it works for partially observed problems without any changes

∇θJ(θ) ≈ (1/N) Σi ( Σt ∇θ log πθ(ai,t | oi,t) ) ( Σt r(si,t, ai,t) )

Variance and Baselines

The problem with policy gradient is that with a small number of samples the variance is really high and we dont get good results, as the number of samples goes to infinity it would give the right answer but we never have infinite samples, like if every trajectory gets a positive reward then every action gets pushed up just by different amounts and the gradient jumps around a lot

The fix is to subtract a baseline b from the reward so that the policy increases the probability of actions that did better than average and decreases the ones that did worse than average

∇θJ(θ) ≈ (1/N) Σi ∇θ log pθ(τ) [ r(τ) − b ]     b = (1/N) Σi r(τ)

But are we even allowed to do that, yes coz the baseline part has zero expected value

E[∇θ log pθ(τ) b] = ∫ ∇θpθ(τ) b dτ = b ∇θ ∫ pθ(τ) dτ = b ∇θ 1 = 0

so subtracting a baseline is unbiased in expectation and only changes the variance, the average reward isnt the best baseline but it is pretty good, if you actually write out the variance and set its derivative wrt b to zero you get the optimal one where g(τ) = ∇θ log pθ(τ)

b = E[ g(τ)2 r(τ) ] / E[ g(τ)2 ]

which is just the expected reward weighted by gradient magnitude, and it is a different baseline for every parameter so in practice people mostly stick with the average

Policy Gradient Is On Policy

Policy gradient is on policy which means if you change the params you have to go back to step 1 and sample again with the new policy, you cant reuse old samples coz the gradient is an expectation under the current pθ(τ) and samples from an old policy come from a different distribution, and since neural nets only change a little bit each gradient step you end up collecting a whole new batch for one tiny update which is really inefficient when sampling is expensive

The way around it is importance sampling which lets you compute an expectation under one distribution using samples from another

Ex ~ p(x)[f(x)] = ∫ q(x) (p(x) / q(x)) f(x) dx = Ex ~ q(x) [ (p(x) / q(x)) f(x) ]

So if we have samples from some other p̄(τ) we can write J(θ) = Eτ ~ p̄(τ)[ (pθ(τ) / p̄(τ)) r(τ) ] and when you write the ratio out the initial state and the transitions cancel coz they are the same in both, so the ratio only needs the policies Πt πθ(at | st) / Πt π̄(at | st) which we know

The Off Policy Policy Gradient

Doing the same thing for new params θ′ using samples from θ gives

∇θ′J(θ′) = Eτ ~ pθ(τ) [ (pθ′(τ) / pθ(τ)) ∇θ′ log πθ′(τ) r(τ) ]

and if you plug in θ = θ′ the ratio is 1 and you get back the normal policy gradient which is a nice sanity check, and using causality ie future actions dont affect the current weight you can cut down the product to only go up to time t

Eτ ~ pθ(τ) [ Σt ∇θ′ log πθ′(at | st) ( Πt′=1t πθ′(at′ | st′) / πθ(at′ | st′) ) ( Σt′=tT r(st′, at′) ) ]

The issue is that product of ratios which is exponential in T so it either blows up or goes to zero, the trick is to write it per timestep with the state action marginals and just ignore the ratio of state probabilities and keep only the action ratio πθ′(ai,t | si,t) / πθ(ai,t | si,t), this looks like cheating right now but part 5 is all about why it is reasonable as long as the policies stay close

Policy Gradient in Practice

To implement it with autodiff you dont compute the gradient by hand, you build a pseudo loss whose gradient is the policy gradient ie the log probs weighted by the Q values and let the framework do the rest

logits = policy.predictions(states)          # (N*T) x Da
negative_likelihoods = softmax_cross_entropy(labels=actions, logits=logits)
weighted_negative_likelihoods = negative_likelihoods * q_values
loss = reduce_mean(weighted_negative_likelihoods)
gradients = loss.gradients(loss, variables)
J̃(θ) ≈ (1/N) Σi Σt log πθ(ai,t | si,t) Q̂i,t

Some tips, remember the gradient has high variance so it isnt like supervised learning and the gradients are going to be really noisy, use much larger batches than you think, and tweaking learning rates is very hard so adaptive ones like Adam are ok ish and there are policy gradient specific ways to pick the step size which is next

Covariant and Natural Policy Gradient

The thing with θ ← θ + α∇θJ(θ) is that some params change the probabilities a lot more than others, so one fixed step size is too big for some directions and too small for others, a gradient step is really solving argmaxθ′ (θ′ − θ)T∇θJ(θ) subject to ||θ′ − θ||2 ≤ ε which limits how far we move in parameter space but we actually care about how far we move in policy space

So instead we constrain the change in the policy itself using a divergence that doesnt care how it is parameterized, usually KL divergence, and KL can be approximated with the Fisher information matrix F

DKL(πθ′ || πθ) ≈ (θ′ − θ)T F (θ′ − θ)     F = Eπθ [ ∇θ log πθ(a | s) ∇θ log πθ(a | s)T ]

and solving the constrained problem gives the natural gradient

θ ← θ + α F−1 ∇θJ(θ)

The takeaway is that policy gradient can be hard to use coz different params affect the distribution in different ways and the natural gradient fixes that by rescaling, with natural gradient you pick α and with trust region policy optimization (TRPO) you pick ε and solve for α, and conjugate gradient works well for computing F−1∇J without building F, we come back to this properly in part 5
